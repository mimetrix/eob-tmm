F5 TMM · engineering register · the honest half

Building it: where this gets hard.

The proposal says a verified eBPF engine is safe to run inside TMM — F5's data-plane microkernel, with its own scheduler and stacks, bypassing the Linux kernel for all traffic. True, as far as it goes. This page is the other half: the problems it raises — engineering as much as security.

None is a show-stopper: each has a known mitigation and a day-one path. The work to do, not a verdict against doing it.

Accuracy note — 2026-08-13. This page was written as a proposal. The data-plane mechanism described here now runs on a live TMM: a shield is loaded over a socket into an already-running process, armed at a function entry while traffic flows, and disarmed — no rebuild, no restart. Two claims remain genuinely unproven and stay conditional throughout: no CVE has been mitigated on live traffic, and the per-call cost of an armed hook is unmeasured. Where this page still reads “would”, treat the mechanism as built and the outcome as not yet shown.

01 time

A proof that a program terminates is not a promise it fits the budget.

The verifier proves every program halts — it bounds loop iterations. (With --termination enabled — not the default of PREVAIL, the abstract-interpretation eBPF verifier this design uses.) That is not a worst-case execution time (WCET): the longest it can actually take on the hardware. Halting says nothing about fitting the budget at the hook it runs on.

the claim to retire

“It's verified as bounded, so it can't hang the poll loop.”

TMM's poll loop is single-threaded, un-preemptible, run-to-completion — no OS underneath to preempt an overrunning hook, and every hook borrows cycles from the same loop, so one misjudged hook starves every flow on that core.

The fix is two layers — and only one is free.

① at admission · static · once, off-box

The budget pass

Cost the worst-case path through the program's CFG (finite, loops proven-bounded) against the hook's budget — per invocation, gated by the hook's rate: path_class is that rate class (hot per packet, warm per connection or request, cold per exceptional event), and it reads as structure ∧ adversarial reachability — anything an attacker can drive at will is hot wherever it sits in the source. Over budget → reject, fail closed.

runtime cost: none · a build artifact

② at runtime · every execution

The runtime guard

Instruction count is not time — warm cache versus cold changes everything, so something must stop a slow execution stalling the loop as it runs. Irreducible, with a small per-execution cost; which mechanism is the open question below. And note what “terminating” buys alone: PREVAIL's ceiling is 100,000 loop iterations — ~300 µs at ~10 instructions each on a 3 GHz core, so a proven-terminating program can stall the loop for a third of a millisecond. That is the argument for ①.

runtime cost: small, bounded · live machinery

why both

Size settles statically; timing does not. Neither layer needs helpers or a verifier change — the pass, the budget table, the cost model and the guard are new F5 build work.

And the budget divides three ways, not two — the forwarder-versus-proxy dichotomy is too coarse, because TMM contains all three:

the trilemma — the hardest open question here

Enforcing a time bound with no preemption leaves three mechanisms, each costing something we have claimed:

Fuel (a counter at loop back-edges) is cheap and deterministic — but uBPF, the small userspace eBPF interpreter and JIT (just-in-time native compiler) this design embeds, states that its limit “has no effect on JIT'd programs”: fuel means patching uBPF's JIT, costing the “reused as-is” claim on one reused component. (The limit does work in uBPF's interpreter, so an interpreter-only high-assurance build has enforceable fuel with no fork.) Wall-clock reads a clock at back-edges — but on aarch64 that counter ticks at tens of MHz, 10–40 ns granularity against a hot-hook budget of tens of nanoseconds: not measurable at the granularity that matters on half our platforms. Signals/timers cost a syscall per invocation plus delivery jitter — a non-starter here.

So: fuel is the mechanism, not the optional extra, and the wall-clock deadline is reporting rather than enforcement. Decide in the room, not in month nine, which of the three we give up: the run-to-completion loop, the unmodified uBPF, or enforce mode on hot hooks. The good answer is to fork uBPF's JIT for back-edge fuel, own it, upstream it.

what is plausible per packet — ranked, all conditional on measurement nobody has taken

Per-packet observability with a sampling divisor: high confidence — the cost is adjustable downward by construction. Per-packet enforcement on an exceptional branch, time-boxed: good, and where the flagship CVE classes sit — conditional on the fuel guard existing, since an unbudgeted program on an attacker-driven branch is the worst case here. Per-packet enforcement on the common path at line rate: doubtful without a burst-capable invocation form (§05). The contents of an offloaded flow: no — they never enter TMM software (the offload boundary is hookable software; the accelerated middle is not).

Two levers are narrower than they look. A sampling divisor is fine for observability and useless for enforcement: you cannot block one in N instances of an exploit. And time-boxing applies only to mitigation — a few percent for an exposure window is a categorically different trade from a few percent permanently, and does not transfer to always-on telemetry. That window is itself an assumption about operator behaviour: that a patched build cannot always be installed at once.

the problem is also the pitch

Setting those budgets is itself an observability problem — exactly what a few designed-in USDTs (userland statically-defined tracing — probe points built into the source) expose: poll-loop duration per iteration, per-hook execution cost, a stall tripwire. The engine instruments the very loop it runs in.

02 the interface

The VM is the easy 10%. The interface is the other 90%.

What a program sees — the ctx: which fields of the flow, the buffer, the profile — plus the verified helper surface and the map model. That is the real design, and a permanent, versioned ABI once shipped.

the claim to retire

“The base tier is a trivial pure function of ctx.”

The good news, stated precisely: the task is “write the program-type descriptor” — ctx layout, memory regions, helper prototypes — not “modify the verifier.” The no-verifier-fork claim survives; the effort estimate does not.

the interface is the USDT catalog

The designed-in half of that interface is a catalog of well-defined USDTs — one per hook, each a curated ctx — and the other half the per-build typed-argument map that function-boundary probes read. Together they are the ceiling on what the engine can observe or enforce: the catalog bounds the anticipated surface; boundary probes reach any function that survived the build as its own out-of-line body and whose arguments expose the fault. Getting both right is not incidental to the project — it is the project, and the permanent ABI.

day one

  • Ship a minimal ctx per hook + its program-type descriptor — built as a per-core scratch copy, discarded on fall-through, never a live view of TMM state. PREVAIL's descriptor expresses no read-only region, so a verified program can write every byte of its ctx: handed the live argument frame, an observe-only program would be an argument-injection primitive. The copy is the cost, and the copy is what to measure.
  • Start from what's already there — no blank page. TMM's code already holds the state these USDTs expose: the connection table, the TLS record layer, the L7 parser state, the bd/plugin internals, the poll-loop counters. So the first USDTs are a curated window onto structures that already exist, and can begin the day the engine lands.

Deferred: helpers. Each one is a new ABI to secure and a new verifier prototype — ctx-first, helpers-later.

03 shared state

TMM is not one kernel. It's N core-pinned kernels with their own state fabric.

Multiple TMM instances per box (CMP — clustered multiprocessing, one TMM pinned per core), plus connection mirroring to an HA peer, make map and state semantics harder than Linux's single-kernel case, not easier.

the claim to retire

“Maps work like they do in Linux.”

day one

  • When the helper tier lands (deferred): per-CPU maps only — no cross-TMM sharing. The base tier registers no helpers, so it has no map access.
  • State that must survive failover rides TMM's existing mirroring channel — don't reinvent HA replication in the map layer.

Deferred: shared, writable maps across TMMs — they need an explicit concurrency and reconciliation model.

04 the trust surface

The JIT emits native code into the crown-jewel process, and the verifier decides what reaches it.

PREVAIL runs outside TMM — in F5's build/admission pipeline, never in TMM's address space and never on attacker-supplied input — yet an unsound verifier or a buggy JIT still means arbitrary native code in the data plane: the biggest risk class eBPF carries, relocated into TMM.†

the point

The perimeter is the signing gate, not the verifier. Only F5-signed bytecode ever reaches the verifier or JIT — attacker-controlled input never touches them. So a soundness bug is not a traffic-borne RCE — remote code execution triggered purely by sending traffic through the data path, needing no credentials and no management-plane access; it's a supply-chain concern gated by signing-key protection.

attacker-controlled bytecode → signing gate✗ rejected
F5-signed bytecode → verify → JIT✓ runs

And verified ≠ correct. The proof covers memory-safety and termination, not intent — a verified, signed program can still black-hole traffic, so blast radius needs its own bound.

day one

  • Signing gate + HSM-backed key protection — a hardware security module holding the signing key. This is the real perimeter; protect it accordingly.
  • JIT hardening (W^X, guard pages) and an interpreter-only high-assurance build option — trade JIT speed for a smaller native-code surface.
  • A control-plane canary that auto-unloads on a health signal — the proof bounds crashes, the canary a bad-but-valid program.
we're not alone on this

“How do you know the verifier is sound?” has no satisfying answer of the form it's widely used. What works is inspectability — tooling that steps through the abstract interpretation so a reviewer can see why a program was accepted, and so the verifier can be unit-tested against adversarial input. That is the form soundness evidence has to take for a TMA (Threat Model Analysis — F5's formal security review, gating here). Establish early whether PREVAIL offers it: the signing gate bounds who could exploit a soundness bug, but only auditability reduces the chance there is one.

† Sharper still given source-code exposure — an adversary holding the code can hunt soundness bugs directly. Which is the point: secrecy was never the defense; the signing gate is. The perimeter holds whether or not the source is public.

05 further concerns

Thirteen more to get ahead of — and one of them is the biggest item here.

The four above are the ones the design turns on; these are the next tier. Read the list with its severity in mind, because thirteen equal-looking boxes overstate the risk. Exactly one is both unavoidable and hard — the safe point, and it is not one thing: it is an ordered publish that needs no rendezvous, a rendezvous needed only to change live text, and quiescence that has three answers. Two are not open questions at all: keeping the verifier out of TMM is a decision already taken, and the concurrency item records a property that makes three other things simple. Four are ordinary lifecycle and governance work. And four exist only because function-boundary probes are on the table — the build-specific probe context, the optimiser deciding the hookable set, the unwind information a trampoline needs, and the JIT’s maturity. Take the narrowest form of the mechanism, designed-in call sites only (outcome 3 of the measurement), and those four go away along with live-text patching — and so does the rendezvous. What remains is ordered publish, a reclamation policy, certification, and the ordinary work.

item zero · the safe point — which is three items

Every in-TMM piece once assumed “a safe point between poll-loop iterations that picks up a load request.” Two things were wrong with that. Nothing needs picking up in the poll loop: parse, verify and JIT compile run on a control thread — as first sketched they ran at the safe point, milliseconds in which the loop is not polling, a latency spike and possibly a dropped heartbeat. What reaches the hot path is one ordered store into a slot it already reads. And the rest is separable: a rendezvous, needed only to modify live text — x86-64 only, since aarch64’s aligned NOP↔B swap is safe against concurrent execution — and available either from the poll loop or from a userspace text_poke_bp equivalent; and quiescence, to free a program’s code, from a poll-loop epoch, from per-invocation read-side markers, or by declining to free and capping the leak. The poll loop is the cheapest source of the last two, not the only one — every alternative pays in hot-path cycles, a restart, or leaked memory. Exactly one capability has no substitute: an atomic multi-slot change. TMM is run-to-completion per packet, so a checkpoint between iterations is the only point where several slots flip with no core seeing a half-applied set — without one, a packet already past hook A sees old-A/new-B and no hot-path counter can rewind it. That is forfeited rather than blocking, since day one is one program per hook with chaining refused. It was not on the list at all, and when added it was added as one item.

certification · FIPS / Common Criteria

An appliance validated under FIPS 140-2/3 (crypto-module validation) or Common Criteria (independent evaluation against a stated target) that also loads data-plane code at runtime is a certification problem — evaluators may not accept “it's signed,” which can force a dynamic-load-disabled certified mode. Likely the biggest productization gate.

keep the verifier out of TMM

PREVAIL (heavy C++/Boost) verifies at build / control-plane time; only the small runtime and a signature check live in TMM. This shrinks the §04 surface.

boundary-probe ctx is build-specific

A function-boundary probe's contract — function X's typed args at build Y — is regenerated every build: more reach than a versioned USDT ctx, looser contract. The signed hook map and the binding's build range keep it honest.

uBPF JIT maturity

§04 is really “the verifier and this JIT.” uBPF's JIT is far less battle-tested than the kernel's — audit / harden / fork it, or default to the interpreter.

the optimiser decides the hookable set

The flag pads the entries of functions the build emits. At -O2 that is a subset of the source: an inlined function has no entry of its own (pushing the boundary out to its caller — a wider skip radius, not lost reachability), -fipa-icf folds identical bodies so arming one arms the other, and clones (.constprop) change both symbol and argument list. So the hookable set is knowable per build but is not the set of functions you can name from reading the code, and pinning a specific one into it means noinline — a source change. Publish the set as a build artifact and treat it as the contract.

invocation granularity · per-packet vs. batched

eBPF's calling convention takes one ctx, once — it cannot express “here is a vector of 256 packets,” so a data plane whose speed comes from a stage seeing a whole batch is a poor host for bytecode. TMM is the favorable case: a proxy, run-to-completion and core-pinned, per-flow rather than per-vector. What remains is per-invocation overhead where TMM does batch (burst receive): a burst-capable invocation form, with §01's budget pass reasoning per burst — which §01 ranks as the enabling mechanism for common-path enforcement, not merely a concern.

handlers running at the same time

A different question from arming, and three simplifications rest on it. Within one instance there is no concurrency at all — single-threaded, un-preemptible, run-to-completion — which is why per-core counters need no atomics, one ctx scratch buffer per core suffices, and a per-core depth guard answers re-entrancy completely. Across instances there is full N-way parallelism: arming a hook arms it everywhere, so N copies run at once on disjoint flows — hence shared state is a real concurrency problem, STATUS must report per-instance (skew is diagnostic — disaggregation picks the instance), and partial arming is not “mostly protected”. The one real seam is the loader mutating a slot while that slot’s program runs on another core: defined memory ordering on those fields, and epoch-based quiescence before freeing a VM.

many programs armed at once

The support answer first: different hooks armed at once, yes; two chained on one hook, refused; two handlers at the same instant on one core, impossible. Beyond that, every argument here is written for one program at one hook — arm two and four things appear. (1) One slot per hook, so a second load on an armed hook gets a distinct rejection, not a silent overwrite — which would disarm a live shield and reset its counter, looking exactly like “no matches.” (2) Chaining is deferred because the blocker is declaring a total order over the outcome set, not plumbing: without one, behaviour follows load order, which is config-sync arrival order and differs across HA peers. (3) Nothing bounds the cumulative armed cost: the budget is per hook, and hooks on a flow’s path are not independent, so a flow pays the sum of the hooks it traverses, not the maximum. (4) Worst of them: a shield that safe-returns — the host returning a benign value in place of the whole body — past a body containing another hook silences it, and that hook reads zero fires, indistinguishable from “nothing matched.” Our own mitigation manufacturing a false success, so a masked hook reports masked, never zero.

multi-tenancy · partitions · vCMP

BIG-IP is deeply multi-tenant, and vCMP guests (virtual CMP — hardware-partitioned guests) each run their own TMM. A program's scope, authorization, and blast radius must be tenant-aware from day one.

ISSU + failover

ISSU (in-service software upgrade) and HA failover need defined behavior: loaded programs re-verify and reload on the new TMM, with explicit map-state handling. (Extends §03.)

post-mortem debuggability — it cuts both ways

The first concern support engineers raise. A backtrace through a hooked function is garbage without correct CFI on the trampoline — the call-frame information an unwinder walks. A PC inside JIT’d code lands in anonymous memory with no symbol unless the loader registers the region. And the one rarely listed: a core dump has to record which shields were armed, with hashes, hooks and modes — without that, every future crash on a box that has ever loaded one is ambiguous, a support cost paid even on crashes no shield touched. Patched text also means the dump no longer matches the shipped binary. The flip side: a flight recorder captures the run-up into a fault, which a dump structurally cannot — but it does not replace core dumps, because a probe only answers a question someone already thought to ask.

jitter-sensitive deployments

Trading, 5G UPF and similar won't tolerate any added per-packet jitter — expect a per-hook / per-deployment opt-out.

07 the reality

and the honest size of it

An earlier draft described this as “hundreds of lines, not subsystems.” Reviewed against what each item requires, a defensible v1 on two CPU architectures is subsystem-scale work, not a feature, plus the TMA and certification engagement. No month or headcount figure is offered: a proposal, not a plan. The items that grew most are the safe point (unlisted), arm/disarm (live-text patching), and the hook-map generator — a parameter classifier over DWARF (the compiler's debug-info format) against an optimised build, the least-proven engineering assumption in the package.

The reframe that matters: the subsystem being added is not the VM. It is a code-patching, live-text, dynamic-code-loading facility inside the crown-jewel process, with its own build-pipeline toolchain and a permanent per-build ABI. That is a subsystem — but describing it as smaller than it is doesn't make it easier to say yes to; it makes the yes collapse in month nine. So this register argues for a sequencing, not a commitment: three questions decide whether the rest is worth designing, and none requires building the engine. Measure the compiler flag twice — the idle cost with nothing armed, then the armed cost at rate — settle a ctx model that verifies against real TMM debug info, and arm one hook end-to-end in a lab TMM with core dumps still readable.

The verifier is the floor, not the building.

It gives you memory-safety and termination — not WCET, not correctness, not immunity from its own bugs. The engine is defensible because the signing gate keeps attacker input away from the verifier and JIT, a budget + fuel guard bounds execution time, and a canary bounds a valid-but-bad program. Verification is one layer of several.

This design is not self-certifying. Everything here is engineering input to a formal TMA by F5 security — a gating prerequisite before implementation, not a formality. The trust surface (§04) in particular must be threat-modeled and signed off. This register is what to bring to that review, not a substitute for it.
